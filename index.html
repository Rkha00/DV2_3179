Child development and learning in Australia

More Children Seen

Australia is identifying far more children as autistic or with ADHD than it did ten years ago. This page follows those children: how many there are, who they are, where they live, and what support reaches them.

1. How common 2. Over time 3. Who 4. Where 5. Support 6. What it means

1

How common is it?

4.3%

of Australian children aged 5–14 were autistic in 2022, up from 2.8% in 2015. That is roughly one child in every classroom of 25.

Autism is a lifelong difference in how a person communicates, interacts and experiences the world.

ADHD (attention deficit hyperactivity disorder) affects attention, activity levels and impulse control.

Developmental delay means a young child is reaching milestones, such as talking or walking, later than expected.

So how did we get from 28 to 43 in every 1,000 in just seven years?

2

Awareness over time

Both autism and ADHD are being identified far more often than a decade ago. Much of this rise is thought to reflect greater awareness and better diagnosis, rather than a real change in how many children have these conditions.

Girls' ADHD rate grew fastest. Is the same thing happening with autism?

3

Who is diagnosed?

Boys are still far more likely to be identified, but girls are catching up: between 2018 and 2022 the number of autistic females almost doubled (up 96%), while autistic males rose by 24%. Age matters too, because some labels are only used for the youngest children.

Most of these children get support through the NDIS. But does it reach them equally across Australia?

4

Where is support reaching?

The National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) funds support for children with a lasting disability or a delay in their development. To compare places fairly, the maps below count children on the scheme per 1,000 children living there, rather than raw numbers, so large states do not look worse just because more children live there.

Being on the scheme is one thing. What support do these children actually receive?

5

What support do children get?

Each child on the NDIS has a plan with a yearly budget. Budgets depend on how much day-to-day help a child needs, so they vary a lot between disability groups.

6

What this means

43 in 1,000

children aged 5–14 are autistic, up from 28 in 2015. Recognition is rising quickly, especially for girls.

2×

A child in South Australia is about twice as likely to be on the NDIS for autism or developmental delay as a child in the Northern Territory.

48%

of autistic students who get support at school say they still need more.

More children are being seen. The next step is making sure support keeps up, wherever a child lives and whatever label they are given. If you are a parent, teacher or carer, the NDIS early childhood partners and your child's school are good first places to ask about support.

Rates per 1,000 children. NDIS counts of children aged 0–14 were divided by the ABS estimated resident population aged 0–14 in each state (June 2025) and multiplied by 1,000.

Privacy-protected counts. The NDIS reports some small counts as "less than" a number (for example "<11" or "<948") to protect privacy. Counts shown as "<11" were treated as zero; larger ones were treated as one less than the number shown. State totals use the NDIS published totals, and the estimates are within 2% of them.

Disability groups. NDIS groups were combined into six groups: developmental delay includes global developmental delay; intellectual disability includes Down syndrome; hearing, vision or speech combines the sensory groups; physical or neurological includes cerebral palsy, acquired brain injury and other physical or neurological conditions.

Survey data. Autism figures come from the ABS Survey of Disability, Ageing and Carers, run every three to four years; 2022 is the latest release. Survey estimates have a margin of error, especially for small groups.

Map projection. Maps use an equal-area (Albers-type conic) projection, so each state's area on screen is true to its real area.
